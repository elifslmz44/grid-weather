Can Britain's electricity grid
reveal the weather?

An experiment in extracting environmental signals from electricity demand — without ever showing the model the temperature.

The country's electricity demand rises and falls in a daily pulse. Hidden inside that rhythm is the weather. This is an attempt to read it back out.

↓

01

Britain has an electrical heartbeat

Every day the grid traces nearly the same shape: a deep overnight trough, a steep morning ramp as the country wakes, and an evening peak when people come home. Winter lifts the whole curve; summer flattens it. None of this mentions temperature yet — it is pure behaviour and physics, the raw material the experiment will work from.

The working week leaves its own mark. Weekday mornings start hard; weekends sleep in. That gap has nothing to do with the weather — and separating this behavioural rhythm from the genuine weather signal is the core challenge of everything that follows.

02

There are hidden frequencies

Treat a decade of half-hourly demand as one long waveform and take its Fourier transform, and the rhythms resolve into sharp spikes. Each spike is a cycle the country repeats: a dominant 24-hour beat, a weekly cycle from the working week, and — the one that matters here — a large annual swing. That yearly component is, in large part, the heating season written directly into the grid.

03

Hide the thermometer

Here is the rule that makes this an experiment rather than a correlation plot: the model never sees the temperature. It is trained to infer daily temperature from electricity behaviour and the calendar alone. Even solar and wind generation are withheld — they are effectively sunshine and wind measurements, and letting them in would be cheating.

      When the grid lies

      07

      What did we actually learn?

      Britain's electricity demand does carry a real temperature signal — but as a supplement to the calendar, not a replacement for it.

      The relationship is also asymmetric: the grid reads cold far better than heat, because Britain heats partly with electricity but rarely cools with it. And it is correlation, not measurement — demand responds to temperature, daylight, behaviour and the calendar all at once, and those are deeply entangled. A grid is not a thermometer. But it remembers the weather more than you might expect.

      Honest limitations

      • Weather is ERA5 reanalysis (a model blend), a documented stand-in for Met Office station data.
      • Recent NESO weeks are provisional and revised in arrears.
      • Demand fell over the decade; the models must not read that trend as a season clock.
      • Calendar features can encode season directly — which is why Model A and B are kept separate.
