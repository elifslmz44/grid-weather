FIELD NOTES // WHY I BUILT THIS

I like treating everyday systems as instruments, and a power grid is about the biggest one there is — a country-sized sensor. Every kettle, heat pump and factory shift leaves a mark in the demand curve, and the weather is one of the strongest forces pushing on it. So I gave myself a rule that turned it into a real experiment: hide the thermometer, and see whether a decade of electricity data alone could tell me what the weather had been.

The interesting part was never a clever model — it was doing the whole thing properly: real data pulled from public APIs, a pipeline that survives daylight-saving and gaps in the record, and an honest test on years the model never saw, one that refuses to flatter the result. What follows is what the grid actually revealed. — Elif

MODULE 01 // ELECTRICAL HEARTBEAT

Britain has an electrical heartbeat

Every day the grid traces nearly the same shape: a deep overnight trough, a steep morning ramp as the country wakes, and an evening peak when people come home. Winter lifts the whole curve; summer flattens it. None of this mentions temperature yet — it is pure behaviour and physics, the raw material the experiment will work from.

FIG.01 — mean National Demand by time of day (local), all year and by season.

The working week leaves its own mark. Weekday mornings start hard; weekends sleep in. That gap has nothing to do with the weather — and separating this behavioural rhythm from the genuine weather signal is the core challenge of everything that follows.

● REC TELEMETRY LOG · 04 JUL 1990

The 2,800 MW cup of tea

The largest demand surge in British history wasn't industrial — it was a kettle. When the England v West Germany World Cup semi-final went to penalties, the nation waited, then rose as one to boil the kettle: a 2,800 MW spike in minutes, roughly 1.1 million kettles at once. "TV pickup" is so predictable that grid engineers pre-position reserves for it. Behaviour, written straight into the load — exactly the signal this project reads.

MODULE 02 // FREQUENCY DOMAIN

There are hidden frequencies

Treat a decade of half-hourly demand as one long waveform and take its Fourier transform, and the rhythms resolve into sharp spikes. Each spike is a cycle the country repeats: a dominant 24-hour beat, a weekly cycle from the working week, and — the one that matters here — a large annual swing. That yearly component is, in large part, the heating season written into the grid.

FIG.03 — power spectrum of demand (log period axis). Peaks = cycles the grid repeats.

TELEMETRY LOG · ELIDIR FAWR, WALES

The mountain that answers in 16 seconds

To absorb surges like TV pickup, the grid keeps a battery the size of a mountain. Dinorwig — "Electric Mountain" — hides six turbines inside a Welsh peak and can throw 1,728 MW onto the grid from a standstill in about 16 seconds, the fastest-responding plant in Britain. The whole system runs on a knife-edge at 50 Hz; that tight balance is why demand tracks reality so faithfully.

TELEMETRY LOG · LONG-TERM TREND

The incredible shrinking grid

Britain's electricity demand has been quietly falling — by roughly – every year over the decade, as LED lighting, better efficiency and rooftop solar eat into it. The models have to be careful not to mistake that slow decline for a change of season — which is exactly why the long-term trend is measured and removed.

MODULE 03 // TEMPERATURE RESPONSE

The shape of the relationship

Before hiding the thermometer, it's worth seeing the relationship the whole project exploits. Plot each day's average demand against its average temperature and a clear signature appears: demand climbs steeply as it gets colder, then flattens once the weather turns mild. Britain heats with electricity but rarely cools with it — so the curve has a kink, not a symmetric V. The fitted breakpoint is where heating demand switches on.

MODULE 04 // EXPERIMENT DESIGN

Hide the thermometer

Here is the rule that makes this an experiment rather than a correlation plot: the model never sees the temperature. It is trained to infer daily temperature from electricity behaviour and the calendar alone. Even solar and wind generation are withheld — they are effectively sunshine and wind measurements, and letting them in would be cheating.

      MODULE 06 // VALIDATION & UNCERTAINTY

      Does it hold up, and how sure is it?

      A single train/test split can get lucky. So the model is re-tested with walk-forward validation: train on every year up to some point, predict the next year it has never seen, then step forward and repeat. If the error stays low and steady across all those years, the result is real — not an artefact of one convenient split.

      ▮ grid model ▮ calendar baseline

      Error on each year the model never trained on (walk-forward). Lower is better.

      A responsible estimate also carries its uncertainty. Calibrating on the model's own out-of-sample misses gives a prediction interval — a band the true temperature should fall inside most of the time.       TELEMETRY LOG · 19 JUL 2022 · 40.3 °C

      Why the grid feels cold but not heat

      When Britain broke 40 °C for the first time in 2022, demand barely flinched — because the country heats with electricity but almost never cools with it. A cold snap sends demand surging as heaters and lights switch on; a heatwave does little, since air-conditioning is rare in UK homes. That asymmetry is visible in this project's own numbers: the grid reads cold far more sharply than heat.

      MODULE 09 // FAILURE ANALYSIS

      When the grid lies

      The model's biggest mistakes are the most revealing part of the study. On these days electricity behaviour broke its own rules — and the usual culprits are not weather at all, but the calendar behaving strangely: Christmas, New Year, bank holidays, when the country stops acting like itself. (Christmas Day is reliably one of the lowest-demand days of the entire year.)

      MODULE 10 // DEMAND FORECAST

      Where is demand heading?

      Everything so far looked backwards — reconstructing the past. This looks forward: a transparent seasonal-trend model (long-term trend + annual cycle + working week + holidays) forecasts national demand for the next two months. The error band comes from a walk-forward backtest: how the model did on days it hadn't seen, rather than in-sample residuals.

      SCOPE & LIMITS

      • This forecasts demand, not weather. It reads the calendar — season, weekday, holidays, trend — but not an upcoming cold snap.
      • A production forecaster would feed in a numerical weather forecast; this is a strong, interpretable baseline, not that.
      • The band widens in reality during unusual weather; the stated coverage is the historical backtest average.

      MODULE 11 // DATA MODEL

      Under the hood: a SQL warehouse

      The daily figures don't have to live in pandas. The same transformations are also expressed as a small star schema in SQL (DuckDB) — one conformed date dimension, two daily fact tables — built straight off the tidy CSVs. It's the difference between a script and a queryable model.

      A transformation layer is only trustworthy if it agrees with the source of truth, so it's reconciled: the SQL daily aggregation is checked against the pandas one, day for day.

      SELECT season, ROUND(AVG(nd_mean),0) AS mean_demand_mw, COUNT(*) AS days
      FROM v_daily GROUP BY season ORDER BY mean_demand_mw DESC;

      A live query against the star schema: mean demand by season.

      MODULE 13 // CONCLUSION

      What did we actually learn?

      Britain's electricity demand does carry a real temperature signal — as a supplement to the calendar, not a replacement for it.

      The relationship is asymmetric (the grid reads cold better than heat) and it is correlation, not measurement — demand responds to temperature, daylight, behaviour and the calendar all at once, and those are deeply entangled. A grid is not a thermometer. But it remembers the weather more than you might expect.

      SYSTEM NOTES · LIMITATIONS

      • Weather is ERA5 reanalysis (a model blend), a documented stand-in for Met Office station data.
      • Recent NESO weeks are provisional and revised in arrears.
      • Demand fell over the decade; the models must not read that trend as a season clock.
      • Calendar features can encode season directly — which is why Model A and B are kept separate.
